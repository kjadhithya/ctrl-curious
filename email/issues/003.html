>  Today, in brief
Nielsen Norman Group put 120 synthetic respondents against 604 real professionals and found they reach the same headline answer but collapse into a dozen labels where humans gave fifty, a sharp warning for anyone using AI personas in discovery. Paul Boag argues in Smashing that most teams are rolling out AI like a software licence instead of a culture change, and Jakob Nielsen adds that AI now generates faster than people can review, so interfaces should offer a few distinct directions first. From Bengaluru, Dylan Field used Config India to launch Figma Specs and code layers and tell builders not to hand their thinking to AI. And Google is rolling out a rebuilt Translate app on Android with bottom navigation and a much bigger input card.

>  Skill of the week
Name components so people and agents find them
With agents and MCP reading your library, a component name is now an API. Vitaly Friedman's guide to naming in design systems gives a practical way to settle names before they spread into code.
01Before naming or renaming, check what other systems call the same thing in Component Gallery, which collects names for more than 50 components.
02If you can describe the pattern but not name it, look it up by how it looks or behaves in Name That UI.
03Choose the word your engineers and users already use, not one team's internal jargon.
04Keep a naming backlog of conflicts and resolve them on purpose, since muddled names slow adoption.
05Keep names short, structured and consistent, and describe purpose rather than visual properties.
 Our take  The steps are our ordering of Friedman's advice. Our own addition: run your top ten names past an agent with your MCP connected and see whether it picks the right component without help.
 
>  Prompt of the week
Break a design deadlock with hybrids
When a team is stuck between two directions, Jakob Nielsen suggests letting AI lay out both positions plus hybrids, so the group critiques a spread of options instead of defending one. This prompt keeps the output small enough to review.
Prompt  ·  Any AI assistant
> You are a senior product designer helping a team that is stuck between two directions for [screen or flow]. Direction A: [describe or attach] Direction B: [describe or attach] Users and context: [who, where, constraints such as one hand or poor signal] 1. State the strongest case for A and for B in three bullets each, including the assumption each one rests on. 2. Propose up to 8 hybrids. For each: one-line idea, what it takes from A and from B, and the assumption it tests. 3. Critique all 10 against [goal metric] and [two heuristics or accessibility needs], in a table. 4. Recommend the 3 most distinct options to prototype. Do not add visual detail yet.
Attach screenshots where you can. Ask for detail only after the team picks a direction, and time the whole loop, generation plus review, against how long the debate was taking.
Use it for
›  Two strong directions and a stalled review
›  Getting quieter people to critique options nobody owns
 
Not for
›  Choices that need real user evidence to settle
›  Replacing a usability test of the final candidates
