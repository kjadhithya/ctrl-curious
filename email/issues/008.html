>  Skill of the week
Measure what AI really saves your team
NN/g's survey of 521 UX practitioners found AI makes tasks feel much shorter, yet sentiment is only mildly positive, largely because checking unreliable output eats the gains. Its recommendations add up to an audit you can start this week.
01List the tasks your team now uses AI for and split them into work with users (interviews, testing designs) and internal work (synthesis drafts, copy, specs). The savings were smallest for work with users.
02For two weeks, log the full time per task: prompting, checking and fixing the output, plus the time teammates spend cleaning it up downstream.
03Judge the result by what reaches users, sooner or better, not by how many people use AI.
04Decide where saved time goes. Respondents who reinvested it in the work, like more exploration or testing, saw quality gains; those who turned it into more output did not.
05Bring the numbers and the open concerns, such as mandates and ethics, to a retro, and treat them as data rather than resistance.
 Our take  The two-week log and the retro are our suggestion; the split by task type, the system-level view and the advice on reinvesting saved time come from NN/g. Keep the sample in mind: it is a convenience sample of NN/g's own audience.
 
>  Prompt of the week
Audit the expand and collapse controls in your UI
Vitaly Friedman's roundup of accordion icon research found that missing or custom icons make people expect a new page, while a caret pointing down when closed is the safest choice. This prompt turns those findings into a quick audit of your own screens.
Prompt  ·  Any AI assistant that reads images
> You are a senior interaction designer auditing expand and collapse controls. Screens: [attach screenshots of accordions, FAQs, filters and menus, closed and open] Platform and audience: [web or app, who uses it] Check every collapsible item against these rules: 1. It has an indicator. With no icon, people expect a link to a new page. 2. The indicator is a caret pointing down when closed and up or sideways when open, or a plus and minus. Flag custom icons and right-pointing arrows. 3. One control does one thing. Flag rows where the label opens a page and the icon expands the section. 4. Items that go to a page are underlined links, with no arrow. 5. A plus sign isn't also used nearby for add, save or zoom. Return a table: item, screen, rule broken, why it confuses people, suggested fix. Then list the three fixes with the biggest impact. Don't redesign anything else.
Attach both the closed and open state of each item. Treat the output as a list of hypotheses and check the top three with a quick test on real users before changing the design system.
Use it for
›  FAQ, filter and menu audits before a release
›  Settling an icon debate in design system review
 
Not for
›  Deciding whether content should be collapsed at all
›  Replacing a usability test of complex navigation
 Our take  For a category that also has its own page, follow the DHL pattern the article cites: the row only expands, and an overview link sits inside.
