>  Today, in brief
NN/g's Tanner Kohler read the AI-native development playbooks from the big labs and found they keep UX only as background context, so he lays out where user research drops out and how designers can win it back, from review triggers to owning the context files. His colleague Raluca Budiu argues the field now covers agents acting for people as well as the people themselves, with handoffs between the two as the place things break. On the craft side, Karl Koch shows what a trustworthy undo for AI edits needs, and Den Odell's speed test finds Ionic took over a second to open a 249-item country picker. >  Skill of the week
Write the rules for when AI-built work needs a designer
NN/g's review of AI-native development playbooks found that agents check their output only against the team's own documents, never against how people behave. Its fix is borrowed from engineering: the way risky code changes go to an architect, risky experience changes should go to a designer. Here is how to set that up.
01List the changes that should never ship without a UX look. NN/g's examples are changes to navigation, information architecture and flows that break a convention; add your own, such as anything touching payments or consent.
02Agree with your product owner what a feature request must show before it is approved: evidence the problem is real, that solving it helps users, and that the team understands the cause.
03Ask engineering to watch experience signals next to server errors: task abandonment, conversion drops and spikes in support tickets. A flag means research first, then a new build.
04Write your team's UX standards as context the agents read, and put your name on them, even if the first version is rough.
05Make research findings and the design system machine-readable, so an agent can surface evidence for or against a request while it is still being written.
 Our take  Start with step one: a one-page list of review triggers is easy to agree on and stops the riskiest changes first. Keep it short, or every ticket will end up in your queue.
 
>  Prompt of the week
Check that people can take back an AI edit
Karl Koch's test for any AI feature that changes someone's work is three questions: can they see what changed, keep a useful phrase without taking everything, and go back to their own wording without losing later edits? This prompt runs that test on your flows before users do.
Prompt  ·  Any AI assistant that reads images
> You are a senior interaction designer reviewing an AI feature that edits people's work. Feature: [what the AI changes, e.g. rewrites a note, fills a form, edits a design] Screens: [attach the flow: before, the suggestion, after accepting, and any undo or history screen] Who else can see the result: [only the author, teammates, customers] Check the flow against these questions: 1. Can the person see the proposal next to the original, not just the new version? 2. Are there clear, separate actions to accept, keep the original or edit the suggestion? 3. Can they keep part of the suggestion without accepting all of it? 4. After accepting, is the previous version still there and clearly labelled? 5. Is it clear what undo restores, and will it wipe edits made after the AI change? 6. Does recovery depend on a toast that disappears? 7. If others may already have seen the result, does the wording say what restoring will and won't change? 8. Does the friction match the commitment: light for small reversible changes, a preview or confirmation for hard-to-reverse ones? Return a table: question, pass or fail, what you saw, suggested fix. Then name the single change that would most increase people's willingness to try the feature again.
Include the screen after accepting and the history view, since most failures show up there. Treat the result as a checklist for your next design review, and try the three-question test yourself with a deliberately poor suggestion.
Use it for
›  Reviewing rewrite, autofill and agent-edit flows before release
›  Making the case for version history in an AI feature spec
 
Not for
›  Judging whether the AI's output is good
›  Actions that can't be undone, such as sent payments, which need a different pattern
 Our take  Koch's sharpest example is a rewrite that reads neatly but turns an open question into a promise, which only shows up when the original sits beside it.
