>  Skill of the week
Write a research section into your next feature spec
NN/g's Tony Alicea argues that when agents can build a feature in a day, research has to travel inside the spec, so tracking ships with the feature and findings feed the next requirements. A release shows what people did, rarely why. Here is how to try it on one release this week.
01Audit what you can see: list where user signals live (analytics, support tickets, feedback) and which of those you and your coding agents can actually reach.
02Add a short research section to the spec, in plain language and in a file next to the requirements so people and agents can both read it: the expected outcome, signs it worked, signs it caused harm, what to log and when to talk to people.
03Ask the agent to build the logging, dashboard or feedback prompt alongside the feature, then review the results after release and feed them into the next spec.
04Sort each question by job: evaluation checks outcomes against predictions, investigation asks why something odd happened, and discovery looks for needs nobody anticipated, which still needs research not tied to releases.
05Set triggers for qualitative work: a regular cadence, an anomaly in the data, and any hard-to-reverse decision such as pricing or data handling.
 Our take  NN/g's own caveats matter: a faster metric may come from staffing or season rather than your change, and heavy tracking can feel like surveillance. Group logs by team or cohort, not by person, as the article's help-desk example does.
 
>  Prompt of the week
Code open-ended survey answers without losing the quotes
Jakob Nielsen compared five human coders with an AI model on 903 open answers and found agreement good enough for AI to join the coding team, provided a person reviews the themes. His rules turn into a prompt: let categories emerge, allow an unclear label, count respondents not mentions, and keep every count tied to real words.
Prompt  ·  Any AI assistant with a long context window
> You are a senior UX researcher coding open-ended survey answers. Question asked: [the exact survey question] Answers: [paste answers, one per line, each with a respondent ID] Rules: 1. Code inductively: let codes emerge from the text, then group codes into themes. Do not start from a preset list. 2. If an answer is too short or ambiguous to code, label it unclear. Never force it into a theme. 3. Count respondents, not mentions: each respondent counts once per theme. 4. Attach two or three verbatim quotes, with respondent IDs, to every theme. 5. Flag any theme that may merge distinct problems, and suggest how to split it. 6. List rare answers that describe a serious or costly problem, even if only one person said it. Return: - A codebook table: code, definition, theme. - A results table: theme, number and share of respondents, quotes. - The unclear answers, listed for me to read. - Rare but serious answers. - One line reminding me that mentions are not prevalence and frequency is not priority.
Read the unclear and rare answers yourself, and decide theme merges by hand. Save the codebook with the results: if you change the model, the prompt or the categories later, recode earlier waves before comparing trends, as Nielsen advises.
Use it for
›  Open questions in surveys, NPS follow-ups and exit forms
›  Turning a pile of feedback into counts you can trace back to quotes
 
Not for
›  Answers generated by simulated users instead of real respondents
›  Estimating how common a problem is across all users, which needs a targeted question or analytics
 Our take  Nielsen's warning is that a model can launder noise into clean-looking numbers. The unclear label and the quotes on every count are what keep you honest.
