NN/g ran the test: AI designs score higher on synthesized findings than on raw comments. Plus Google's new homepage.
Week ahead edition

Issue 028 · View online
>  Today, in brief
If you paste user comments straight into an AI design tool, NN/g's new study says you are leaving quality on the table: designs built from a researcher's synthesized findings met more criteria than ones built from raw feedback, and only the synthesized ones remembered loyalty miles. Jakob Nielsen rounds up research showing developers wave through agent permission prompts and proposes which actions should be silent, asked or blocked. On the interface side, Google has swapped its homepage's search button for Gemini shortcuts and a chat-history sidebar, a sign that even the simplest query box is turning into a resumable session. And this week's skill comes from UX Collective: a way to sort dashboards so the fires come first.
 
>  Read
01Can AI generate good designs from raw user data?
Nielsen Norman Group · Megan Chan and Rachel Banawa gave an AI tool the same prompt and design-system context with either about 100 raw comments or a researcher's synthesized findings and recommendations, across a flight-rebooking form and a multistep checkout. Synthesized input met 77.9% of criteria against 69.5%, and no raw-input flight design dealt with loyalty miles. Read it for the case for UX-context design: deciding what interpretation the tool needs before you prompt.
02UX roundup: AI permission requests, recognition-primed decisions, directed editing and misleading defaults
UX Tigers · Jakob Nielsen pulls together a survey of 115 developers in which only 48% read agent permission requests carefully and 21% approve by default, then sketches tiers for what an agent should do silently, ask about with consequences shown, or block. Also inside: a slider-based editing study and guidelines for interfaces experts use at speed. Useful if you are designing any AI feature that asks before it acts.
03The judge used to be the user
UX Collective · Wira Indra Kusuma argues that LLM judges have quietly replaced users as the people who define a correct answer, and recounts how walking support staff through a classifier's errors reshaped half of the next version. His fix is concrete: sit real users down for an hour to read eval failures with you. A good prompt for designers who own AI quality but never see the evals.
04Google's latest home page redesign makes Gemini even more prominent
Engadget · The classic Google Search button gives way to Gemini shortcuts for images, files and brainstorming, and a sidebar now keeps chat history and tasks, rolling out globally. Steve Dent notes a recent-queries list would help ordinary search too. Worth studying as the most-copied homepage on the web shifts from one-off queries to sessions you return to.
05Gemini app rolls out a camera redesign on Android
9to5Google · Gemini's camera no longer takes over the screen: it opens from the plus menu as a draggable, nearly edge-to-edge bottom sheet inside the conversation, with flash and front camera tucked into an overflow by the shutter. A small, concrete pattern to borrow if your product mixes capture with chat, since the conversation stays in view.

>  Skill of the week
Run a fires-first audit on one list or dashboard
Francis Wu shows a 20-row service table sorted by last updated, where the two services that matter most sit in rows 16 and 19. His case, drawn from Taylor's exception principle and the ISA-101 standard for control rooms, is that operational screens should lead with what needs a person and let the healthy rest fade. Here is how to apply it to one screen this week.
01Define severity as two separate things multiplied together: health says what broke, importance (tier, revenue, customers affected) says how much it matters. Sort by that, not by status or recency.
02Keep urgency and importance on separate axes, so a degraded critical item can outrank a failing minor one.
03Collapse everything healthy into a single line: a count, a last-checked timestamp and a way to dig in, so people can trust the quiet.
04Move alerts to where people already work, with restraint and a snooze, so nobody has to keep the screen open to watch it.
05Make resolution visible and lead each problem straight into investigation, while the full table stays one click away.
 Our take  Our suggestion: try it first on an internal admin or ops screen, where you can watch real users triage, before applying it to customer-facing dashboards.
 
>  Prompt of the week
Turn raw feedback into context before you ask AI for a design
NN/g found that AI-generated designs met noticeably more criteria when the tool got a researcher's findings and recommendations instead of the raw comments they came from. This prompt does the synthesis as a separate first step, then gives you a brief and a scorecard to judge what the design tool produces.
Prompt  ·  Any AI assistant with a long context window
> You are a senior UX researcher preparing context for an AI design tool. Product and task: [e.g. rebooking a cancelled flight] Users: [who they are, in one line] Raw feedback: [paste comments, tickets or interview notes, one per line] Step 1. Synthesize: write 5 to 8 findings. For each, give the finding in one sentence, how many comments support it, and two short quotes. Step 2. Recommend: for each finding, write one design recommendation phrased as a requirement the screen must meet. Step 3. Flag what the raw comments imply but nobody says outright, such as consequences users will worry about after the main task (fees, points, data loss). Step 4. Write a pass or fail scorecard of 10 to 15 criteria a reviewer can check on a finished design. Step 5. Write a design brief of under 200 words for the design tool, using only the recommendations, not the raw comments. Return the findings, recommendations, flags, scorecard and brief as separate sections. Mark anything you are unsure of.
Check the findings against the raw comments yourself before using them; this is the interpretation step NN/g says a researcher should own. Then give the design tool only the brief, generate a few variants, and score each against the scorecard.
Use it for
›  Feeding research into AI prototyping tools such as Figma Make, Lovable or Firefly Builder
›  Agreeing on what a good screen must do before anyone generates one
 
Not for
›  Replacing a researcher's synthesis on a high-stakes decision
›  Feedback from simulated users rather than real ones
 Our take  NN/g did not name the model or exact prompt it used, and could not separate the effect of findings from recommendations, so treat the scorecard as your real test.
