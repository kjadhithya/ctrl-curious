>  Case study
Microsoft AI's AIX Creative Studio: Jeff Gipson, with illustrator Chi Michalski · Microsoft Autopilot
The art of playing a supporting role
Autopilot, the agent Microsoft introduced in September, needed character and motion design that helps people understand and direct what it does, but an open-ended AI can't be scripted like an animated film. Jeff Gipson, a former Disney Animation writer and director, borrowed timing and anticipation only where they do a job (showing progress, acknowledging input, flagging an error) and kept the agent from competing for attention. The decision that made it work was dropping a single mascot for a flexible visual language built from a line, which can become illustrations of thinking, searching or sending and show state without implying feelings, so the person, not the AI, stays the main character.
>  How they work
AD
Automattic design writing team
Automattic Design · write-up by Michael Pick
In a month, Automattic's design writing team turned research on the tells of AI writing into two installable agent skills, one for interface copy and one for voiceover scripts. How they built them is a set of habits any team can copy for its own UX copy.
Habits worth copying
 
Make the agent ask about audience, voice and constraints before it writes, and give it a short brand file distilled from real copy examples, which carry more voice than a list of adjectives.
 
Pair a deterministic linter (47 banned words and 33 banned patterns) with human review of the judgement calls it flags, around three rules: no parroting, no repeated sentence structures, and no concrete verbs on abstract ideas.
 
Give every ban list a named owner, date each brand file and re-distil it when the source guide changes, and keep product claims verbatim so the agent can never turn them into bigger promises.
 Our take  Start with the brand file: pick ten lines of copy you are proud of and ten you would never ship, and you have most of what the agent needs. The skills are internal for now, so the steps are ours, drawn from the post.
 
>  Decision toolkit
Hand a design task to AI, or keep a person on it?
Jakob Nielsen's new verification ladder ranks UX tasks by how fast a verdict arrives: rules first, then experts, then users, then the market. Stef Hutka adds a second test: whether the feature should think with people or for them.
Hand it to AI when
›  A rule or checklist can judge the result in minutes, as with contrast, spacing or naming
›  You want many divergent options and will spend your user-testing time choosing between them
›  Your guidelines are written down, which moves the task up the ladder
 
Keep a person on it when
›  The only real verdict comes from users or the market, months later
›  Doing the work is how you build the judgement, as with synthesis and critique
›  The task decides what is worth doing or gets the team aligned
Seen in the wild
Baymard UX-Ray
Reports 95% agreement with human auditors across 346 heuristics because it is built on human-mapped data, while a general model doing open heuristic evaluation reached only slight agreement with experts.
Rule of thumb  If you can't say how, and how quickly, the output will be judged, keep a person on it.
 Our take  The columns are our reading of Nielsen's ladder and advice; the think-with test comes from Hutka's book introduction.
