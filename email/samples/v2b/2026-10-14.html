A design review agenda that ends in decisions
Parvaneh Toghiani, a principal designer at Uber, uses a simple structure to keep reviews from circling. Copy it into your next review deck or doc:
review-agenda.md
Review type: alignment / options / sign-off Pre-read: sent 24 hours before 1. Goals and non-goals (2 min) 2. Decisions already made (1 min) 3. Decision criteria, agreed before any design is shown 4. Options: about three, from practical to ambitious, with our recommendation in the middle 5. Discussion: note feedback live, park side topics Recap: each goal as a question, marked decided or open, with owner and next step
Present for about half the meeting and leave the rest for discussion. Never show more than five options.

>  10-minute brief
The accessibility tree, and why AI agents read it
You design what people see. Browsers also build a second, invisible version of every screen, and it is the one assistive tech and AI agents actually use.
 A structure the browser builds from your page: every element reduced to a role (button, link, heading), a name (“Delete draft”) and a state (expanded, selected, disabled).
Who reads it
1
Screen readers
Read out roles, names and states, and let people jump between headings and landmarks.
 
2
Voice control
People say a control's name to press it, so the name has to include the words they can see.
 
3
AI agents
Browsing agents read the same tree to find and press controls; a vague tree means wrong clicks.
How to design for it
01Name every icon button in your specs by its action, “Delete draft”, not “Trash icon”.
02Make the visible label the start of the accessible name, so voice users can say what they see.
03Annotate heading levels and landmarks (header, navigation, main) in handoff, not just text styles.
04Never show state with colour alone; give it a word, such as Selected, Expanded or Error.
05Use a button for actions and a link for navigation; a styled box with a click handler can vanish from the tree.
Why it matters to you
As agents start booking, buying and filling in forms for people, a screen that only makes sense visually is a screen they can't use. The fixes are the same ones disabled users have needed all along.
